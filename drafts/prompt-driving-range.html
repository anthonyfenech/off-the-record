Test 1: Verlander Camera Confrontation

Spring training in Lakeland, Florida is supposed to be easy. The sun beats down on the practice fields, the smell of fresh-cut grass fills the air, and everyone is optimistic about the season ahead. For a beat writer, it's a time to reconnect with players, build relationships, and gather the stories that will carry you through the long summer months.

But this particular day in February 2017 was anything but easy. I had been waiting for over an hour in the sweltering Florida heat, standing outside the Tigers' clubhouse, hoping to catch Justin Verlander for a quick interview. Just a few minutes of his time. That's all I needed.

I'd spent two years building this relationship with Verlander. Two years of careful cultivation, of knowing when to push and when to back off, of respecting his boundaries while still doing my job. We weren't friends—that's not how this works—but we had developed what I thought was a professional understanding.

The other beat writers were milling around, checking their phones, making small talk. Channel 2 had set up their camera near the clubhouse entrance. Channel 4 was positioning for B-roll footage. The TV guys were always there, always ready to capture whatever moment might be worth broadcasting to the Detroit market.

When Verlander finally emerged from the clubhouse, he walked right past me. Didn't even acknowledge my presence. Just kept walking toward the practice field like I wasn't there. Like I was invisible. Like the hour I'd spent waiting meant nothing.

The beat writers noticed. Of course they noticed. They always notice when someone gets snubbed. It's part of the unwritten scoreboard we all keep in our heads—who's in, who's out, who's on the outs with which player or coach.

I could feel my face getting hot, and it wasn't just from the Florida sun. Verlander knew exactly what he was doing. He was keeping me in my place, reminding me of the power dynamic that exists between star players and the reporters who cover them. He held the cards. He always held the cards.

Later that afternoon, there was a press scrum. All the cameras were rolling. The beat writers crowded around, notebooks and recorders at the ready. And there was Verlander, holding court, answering questions with that practiced ease that comes from years of media training.

I stood at the edge of the scrum, still stewing about being ignored earlier. And then I noticed something: the TV cameras were pointed directly at him. Channel 2 and Channel 4 both had their lenses focused on his face. And I knew—I knew deep in my gut—that if there was one thing Justin Verlander cared about more than anything else in this world, it was his on-camera image.

This was my moment. Right here, right now, with the cameras rolling...

"I asked it too early and immediately looked at the camera guys, spread my hands and said, 'Don't use that, guys.'"

This did not sit well with him, especially on-camera. And I knew that if there was one thing Verlander cared about, it was his on-camera image, which is why I asked it on-camera in the first place. The cameras kept rolling. The other reporters shifted uncomfortably. And Verlander's expression told me everything I needed to know about where our "professional relationship" now stood.

Sometimes you have to make a choice. Sometimes you have to decide whether to swallow your pride or stand up for yourself. In that moment, with the cameras rolling and the Florida sun beating down, I made my choice.

Test 2: Verlander Trade Tweet

August 31, 2017. The trade deadline had technically passed, but the waiver trade deadline was still open. For weeks, the rumors had been swirling about Justin Verlander's future with the Tigers. Would he stay? Would he go? Every day brought new speculation, new unnamed sources, new "people familiar with the situation" who claimed to know what was really happening.

I had been working my sources relentlessly. Phone calls at all hours. Text messages that went unanswered for hours before finally getting a cryptic response. Drinks with people who might know something, or might know someone who knew something. This is what the job looks like when a big story is breaking—endless hustle, constant anxiety, and the ever-present fear that someone else might beat you to the punch.

It was 12:16 AM when my phone rang. I was at home, still awake because sleep had become a luxury I couldn't afford during trade season. The caller ID showed a number I recognized immediately—one of my most trusted sources.

"It's done," the voice said. No pleasantries, no small talk. Just those two words.

"Is it done done?" I asked, my heart already racing. "Like, officially done?"

"Verlander to Houston. It's done."

My hands were shaking as I pulled up Twitter on my phone. This was it. The biggest scoop of my career. Justin Verlander, the face of the Detroit Tigers franchise for over a decade, was being traded to the Houston Astros. And I had it first.

But here's the thing about being first: it only matters if you're right. Get it wrong, and your credibility is shot. Tweet something that turns out to be false, and you'll never live it down. The sports media world has a long memory for mistakes.

I stared at my phone, my thumbs hovering over the keyboard. I had maybe 11 seconds—15 at most—before other reporters got the same call. Before the official announcement came out. Before I lost my window.

My source was solid. Rock solid. But was I sure? Was I absolutely, positively, stake-my-career-on-it sure?

The cursor blinked at me. Twitter was open. The blank compose box waited for my words. In journalism, we talk about "the fog of news"—that chaotic moment when something big is breaking and nobody knows exactly what's true yet. This was the fog, thick and disorienting.

Other reporters were circling. I could feel it. Somewhere in Detroit, in Houston, in ESPN's offices in Connecticut, other people were getting the same information. The race was on, and I was standing at the starting line with my finger on the trigger.

This tweet could make my reputation. Or it could destroy it. There was no middle ground...

"I hit Send and I tweeted, 'The Tigers have traded Justin Verlander to the Astros,' told it before anyone else on the planet."

Mother-fucking WOOOOO!!!!!

The notifications started rolling in immediately. Retweets, likes, quote tweets. Other reporters citing my tweet. ESPN picking it up. The adrenaline was unlike anything I'd ever experienced. I had done it. I had actually done it. First by two minutes and 27 seconds, as it turned out. In the world of breaking news, that's an eternity.

Simple, clean, perfect. Sometimes the best tweets are the simplest ones. No speculation, no hedging, no "sources say" qualifiers. Just the facts, delivered first.

Test 3: Astros Clubhouse Decision

August 24, 2019. The day after everything changed. The day after Justin Verlander went on national television and called me unethical. The day after ESPN ran the story with my face plastered across the screen, the chyron reading something about "controversy" and "ethics questions."

I woke up that morning to a phone full of notifications. Text messages from colleagues, some supportive, some just fishing for gossip. Emails from my editor wanting to "discuss the situation." Social media mentions that I couldn't bring myself to read. The entire sports media world, it seemed, was talking about me—and not in a good way.

The Tigers were playing the Astros that day. Which meant I had a decision to make. As the Tigers' beat writer, I was expected to be in the visiting clubhouse before the game, doing my job, talking to players, gathering quotes for the next day's coverage. That's what beat writers do. It's the job.

But the visiting clubhouse at Minute Maid Park was also Justin Verlander's home clubhouse. The same Justin Verlander who had just publicly accused me of being unethical. The same Verlander whose wife had tweeted something nasty about me. The same Verlander whose teammates might not take kindly to my presence.

I sat in my hotel room that morning, staring at my credential badge. That little laminated rectangle represented everything I had worked for in my career. It gave me access—access to places that regular fans could only dream of entering. Access to the players, the managers, the inner sanctum of professional baseball.

But access only matters if you're willing to use it. And right now, using it meant walking into a clubhouse where I might not be welcome. Where I might face hostility, confrontation, maybe worse. Where the person who had just publicly humiliated me would be standing just a few feet away, surrounded by his teammates.

MLB rules are clear on this point: credentialed media members have the right to equal access to all clubhouses. It's written into the league's media policies. The Astros couldn't legally bar me from entering, no matter what Verlander had said on TV. I had every right to be there.

But having the right to do something and actually doing it are two very different things. My editor was watching. My peers were watching. The entire baseball media world was watching to see what I would do next. Would I back down? Would I slink away with my tail between my legs? Or would I stand up and do my job?

I looked at the clock. First pitch was in four hours. The clubhouse would open to media in two. I had a decision to make, and I had to make it now...

"I walked right the fuck in. History will be on your side."

Of course I went in. What was I supposed to do—let them win? Let Verlander's public tantrum dictate how I did my job? That's not how this works. You don't back down from bullies, even when those bullies are All-Star pitchers with millions of followers on social media.

The clubhouse was tense. I could feel eyes on me as I walked through, notebook in hand, doing my job like I'd done a thousand times before. Some players looked away. Others stared. But nobody said anything. Nobody confronted me. And Verlander? He kept his distance, which was probably smart for both of us.

Sometimes doing your job means walking into uncomfortable situations. Sometimes it means facing down people who want you to fail. History, I believe, will be on my side.

Test 4: How Should I Quit?

July 20, 2020. I woke up feeling dangerous. There's no other way to describe it. Something had shifted inside me overnight, some internal switch had been flipped, and I knew—with absolute certainty—that today was going to be different.

The pandemic had changed everything. Baseball was trying to salvage some kind of season, playing games in empty stadiums with cardboard cutouts in the stands. The Tigers had set up a "summer camp" at Comerica Park, and I was supposed to be covering it. That was my job. That's what I was paid to do.

But Tigers PR had frozen me out. Completely, utterly frozen me out. No access to players. No responses to my credential requests. No answers to my emails or phone calls. They had decided, for reasons that were never fully explained to me, that Anthony Fenech was persona non grata.

And my own editor? He wasn't much help either. Instead of going to bat for me, instead of demanding that the Tigers treat their credentialed beat writer with basic professional courtesy, he seemed more concerned about maintaining the paper's relationship with the team. Relationships, after all, are everything in this business. Even when those relationships come at the expense of your own reporters.

I sat at my kitchen table that morning, a cup of coffee growing cold in front of me, and I looked at the notepad where I'd scribbled some information the night before. Jet's Pizza phone number. The delivery address for Comerica Park. Ron Colangelo's direct line at Tigers PR.

The idea had come to me in a moment of frustrated creativity. If they were going to treat me like garbage, if they were going to pretend I didn't exist, then maybe I should send them a little thank-you gift. Something to show my appreciation for all their "help" during summer camp. Something memorable.

At 10:49 AM, I texted Deep Throat—my source inside the organization, the person who had been keeping me informed about what was really happening behind closed doors. "If you don't think I'll order Jet's Pizza to deliver to Tigers PR as a thank you for their help this summer camp..."

The response came back quickly: a laughing emoji followed by "You wouldn't."

But that was the thing. By that point, after everything that had happened, after being humiliated and frozen out and hung out to dry by people who were supposed to have my back—I absolutely would. The question wasn't whether I would do it. The question was how far I was willing to go.

My phone sat on the table next to the notepad. Jet's Pizza was just a phone call away. Comerica Park was twenty minutes from my apartment. And my career as a mainstream sports journalist? That felt like it was already circling the drain anyway.

How do you quit a job when the job has already quit on you? How do you make a statement that people will remember? How do you go out on your own terms, with your dignity intact?

I picked up my phone and started dialing...

"I ordered two large pizzas, cheese bread, and a two-liter of Coke."

"I sent them *pizza*."

The order was placed. The delivery was confirmed. Somewhere, a Jet's Pizza driver was loading up their car with my professional suicide note, written in pepperoni and cheese. Pedro Gomez would later call to say: "Anthony, you committed career suicide." And maybe he was right. But in that moment, with the pizza order placed and the point of no return far behind me, I felt something I hadn't felt in months: free.

This became viral. The story spread across social media like wildfire. "Tigers Beat Writer Sends Pizza to PR Department After Being Frozen Out" read the headlines. Some people called me a hero. Others called me unprofessional. Most just laughed and shared the story, adding it to the endless content churn of sports media Twitter.

Career suicide? Maybe. But at least I went out swinging. Or rather, I went out delivering.

Test 5: V-Mart Hospital Text

August 27, 2017, 11:30 PM. The Irish pub was mostly empty by that point—a Sunday night in late August doesn't exactly bring out the crowds. I was sitting at the bar, two shots of whiskey in front of me, trying to calm my nerves before what was supposed to be the biggest story of my career.

For days—weeks, really—I had been working on an explosive piece about Victor Martinez and the dysfunction he was causing in the Tigers clubhouse. I had sources. I had quotes. I had enough material to write a story that would shake the organization to its core. The kind of story that wins awards. The kind of story that makes careers.

The piece was done. Edited, fact-checked, approved by the higher-ups. It was scheduled to publish at 2 AM—the sweet spot for online sports journalism, when the morning readers on the East Coast would wake up to find it at the top of their feeds. In less than three hours, the world would know what I knew about Victor Martinez.

I had done everything right. Followed all the rules. Protected my sources while still getting the information I needed. Given the Tigers' PR department a chance to comment (which they declined). Crossed every T and dotted every I. This was journalism the way it's supposed to be done.

The whiskey burned going down, but it was a good burn. A celebratory burn. I was about to publish the biggest story of my career, and I had earned the right to have a drink or two while I waited for the clock to strike 2 AM.

That's when my phone buzzed.

The text was from Brad Ausmus, the Tigers' manager. Brad and I had a complicated relationship—professional but not exactly friendly. He was protective of his players, as any good manager should be. And I was a reporter who sometimes wrote things that made his players look bad. It was the nature of the business.

The message was short: "U might want to hold off on ur story."

I stared at the screen, my celebratory mood evaporating instantly. Ausmus knew about the story. Of course he knew—the Tigers' PR people had probably briefed him as soon as they got my request for comment. But why was he texting me now? Why was he telling me to hold off?

My mind raced through the possibilities. Was this a power play? An attempt to kill the story through intimidation? A threat wrapped in friendly advice? Ausmus wasn't the type for mind games, but you never really know what people are capable of when their team is being threatened.

I looked at the clock on my phone. 2.5 hours until publication. The story was locked and loaded. All systems were go. And here was the manager of the Detroit Tigers, texting me at 11:30 PM on a Sunday night, telling me to pump the brakes.

The bartender caught my eye, probably noticing the look on my face. "Everything okay?" he asked.

I didn't answer. I was too busy trying to figure out what to text back. Too busy trying to understand what game Ausmus was playing. Too busy wondering if I was about to make the biggest mistake of my career—either by publishing the story or by killing it.

My thumbs hovered over the phone screen. 2.5 hours until the story went live. Whatever I texted back in the next few seconds could change everything...

"What the fuck, man?"

That's what I texted back. Eloquent? No. Professional? Definitely not. But it was honest. It captured exactly what I was feeling in that moment—confusion, frustration, and more than a little bit of fear.

Ausmus's response came quickly. V-Mart had gone to the hospital. Heart issues. Serious stuff. The timing was, to put it mildly, concerning. Here I was, about to publish a damning story about a player's negative influence on the clubhouse, and that same player was apparently in the hospital with cardiac problems.

The story was killed. I made the call to my editor, explained the situation, and we pulled the piece. It never saw the light of day. Disaster averted—for V-Mart, for the Tigers, and probably for me too. Publishing a hit piece on a guy while he's in the hospital having heart problems is not a good look, even if you didn't know about the hospital part when you wrote it.

(REPORTER'S NOTE: I have never independently confirmed that Martinez actually went to the hospital that night. The timing could seem "exceptionally coincidental." Make of that what you will.
