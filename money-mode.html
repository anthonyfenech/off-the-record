THE WINTER MEETINGS

SAN DIEGO, December 8—At the winter meetings, hundreds of reporters gather in hotel ballrooms chasing scoops. We're usually cooped up in the media room, with a main stage where press conferences are held, trades announced, and newly inducted Hall of Famers talk about being speechless.

I walk with a purpose, pretending I am a somebody. I stop Ken Rosenthal, as short in person as advertised on Twitter (5-foot-4½), introducing myself. Nice guy.

Each evening, the beat writers are summoned for a media session with the general manager, held in the team's suite. There are five of us, sitting in chairs, while general manager Dave Dombrowski sits at a high-top table on the other side of the room, flanked by assistant Al Avila. Ron Colangelo and Aileen Villarreal (Tigers PR) stand around with on-site executives and scouts.

🔒

I'm startled when I see the gray-haired guy from the flight here. We make eye contact again.

After the session, I realize in horror that I didn't turn my recorder on before I placed it on the table—I have no audio. My competitors have pity, sharing their tape so I'm able to write my stories.

December 10—Back at the lobby bar, I re-introduce myself to a tall, tan man with big shoulders who I recognize from the Tigers' suite—Dave Littlefield, formerly the Pittsburgh Pirates GM, was recently hired as a pro scout.

Embarrassingly, I don't remember our conversation from last night. We talk a bit more.

🔒

I meet Detroit's young analytics guy, Sam Menzin, and another fellow, Matt Something, who works for San Diego. I make a point of meeting agent Dave Pepe in the flesh, a former adjunct professor at Rutgers who represents veteran closer Joe Nathan.

I reached out to Pepe this offseason, had a nice conversation, and I want to pitch an offseason feature story on Nathan, the aging former All-Star who is looking to regain both his fastball and the fans' trust. I suggest I could visit Nathan while training for the season. Pepe buys in.

While it wasn't explicitly stated as such, the story I was pitching to Nathan's agent was a fluff piece. By allowing a certain kind of access, it's almost certain the resulting story will be favorable for the subject.

🔒

December 11—My mouth is dry when I wake up. I get a glass of water and check Twitter. Calamity! My team is close to a trade, reportedly sending a prospect shortstop to the Cincinnati Reds for righty starter Alfredo Simon.

Dave Dombrowski is known for making trades. And by the time I'm nearly finished whipping up a story for the first trade, Dombrowski is reportedly close to making another one—and this one is bigger.

According to Twitter, the Tigers are acquiring outfielder Yoenis Céspedes from the Boston Red Sox. Helplessly, I communicate with the sports desk. Should I re-do the first story to include both trades?

I file the Simon story and start on the Céspedes story. But then I hear about a third trade: the Tigers are sending starting pitcher Rick Porcello to Boston in exchange for Céspedes. I don't know what to make of this.

🔒

I rush downstairs to the media workroom, where a press conference is scheduled. Dombrowski addresses the media, explaining his rationale. I'm in the front row taking notes, trying not to look like I'm about to pass out from the hangover.

The rest of the week goes similarly. More trades, more press conferences, more late nights at the hotel bar. By the time I fly home, I'm exhausted but exhilarated. This is what I signed up for.

SPRING TRAINING

LAKELAND, February 14—The drive from Tampa takes about an hour. I pass strip malls and orange groves, billboards advertising personal injury lawyers and gun shows. Florida is exactly what I expected and nothing like it at the same time.

Joker Marchant Stadium sits on the edge of a lake, surrounded by palm trees and parking lots. The Tigers have trained here since 1966. Before that, they were in Lakeland too, just at a different facility. This town and this team have been intertwined for nearly a century.

🔒

I check into my hotel, a modest place off the highway. The room smells like chlorine from the pool. I drop my bags and head to the ballpark.

Pitchers and catchers reported yesterday. The clubhouse is buzzing. I introduce myself to anyone who will listen, trying to remember names and faces. The veterans eye me warily. The young guys are too nervous to talk. The coaches are busy. The PR staff is overwhelmed.

I find a corner and start taking notes. This is my life now.

* * *

The first few days are a blur of batting practice and bullpen sessions. I learn the rhythms of the place—when the players arrive, when they eat, when they're available for interviews. I learn which coaches will talk and which ones won't. I learn where to stand and where not to stand.

🔒

Miguel Cabrera holds court in the clubhouse, surrounded by reporters. He's the biggest star on the team, a future Hall of Famer, and he knows it. He's gracious with his time but guarded with his words. Everything he says will be dissected and analyzed. He's learned to say nothing while appearing to say everything.

Justin Verlander is different. He's intense, focused, almost unapproachable. When he talks, it's in short, clipped sentences. He doesn't suffer fools. I make a mental note to be very prepared before I ever ask him a question.

🔒

The days blend together. Wake up, drive to the ballpark, watch practice, conduct interviews, write stories, file by deadline, grab dinner, sleep, repeat. It's exhausting and exhilarating. I'm living my dream, even if my dream involves a lot of sunscreen and bad hotel coffee.

February 28—The first spring training game is tomorrow. It doesn't count for anything, but it feels momentous anyway. Baseball is back. Real, actual baseball.

I spend the evening organizing my notes, preparing questions, thinking about storylines. The season is six weeks away. So much can happen between now and then. Injuries, surprises, disappointments. The team that breaks camp won't be the same team that started.

🔒

I fall asleep with my laptop open, the cursor blinking on an unfinished sentence. Tomorrow, the games begin.

OPENING DAY

DETROIT, April 6—The temperature at first pitch is 47 degrees. Not ideal for baseball, but nobody seems to care. Comerica Park is packed. Forty-four thousand people bundled in winter coats, drinking beer and eating hot dogs like it's the middle of July.

I'm in the press box, trying to stay warm and stay focused. Below me, the field looks impossibly green against the gray sky. The Tigers are playing the Twins. It's just one game out of 162, but it feels like so much more.

🔒

Verlander takes the mound for the first pitch. The crowd roars. He winds up, delivers, and the ball smacks into the catcher's mitt. Strike one. We're off.

The game moves slowly, as baseball games do. I take notes, check Twitter, write observations that might become sentences that might become paragraphs that might become a story. The process is the same whether it's April or October, whether the game matters or doesn't.

In the fifth inning, Cabrera launches a home run to left field. The ball disappears into the seats, and the crowd explodes. I watch him round the bases, that familiar trot, and I think about all the home runs he's hit and all the home runs he'll hit. I'm witnessing greatness, even on a cold Monday in April.

🔒

The Tigers win, 4-0. Verlander pitches seven shutout innings. The champagne stays corked—it's just Opening Day—but there's a sense of optimism in the clubhouse. This could be the year. That's what everyone says every year, but sometimes it's true.

I file my story, pack up my laptop, and head home. Tomorrow there's another game, and the day after that, and the day after that. The season stretches out before me like a highway with no end in sight.

🔒

I'm exhausted already, and we're only one game in.

* * *

The first homestand goes well. The Tigers win four out of six. The bats are hot, the pitching is solid, and the defense is making plays. It's early, way too early to draw conclusions, but the vibes are good.

I settle into a routine. I arrive at the ballpark around 2 p.m. for a 7 p.m. game. I check the lineup, talk to the manager, interview a player or two. I grab dinner in the press dining room—usually something beige and forgettable—and then head to my seat.

🔒

After the game, I go to the clubhouse for quotes. The players are tired, sweaty, sometimes irritable. They answer the same questions they answered yesterday and will answer tomorrow. It's part of the job, for them and for me.

Then I write. I write until the words make sense, until the story has a beginning and a middle and an end. I file by deadline, pack up, and drive home through empty streets. I get to bed around 1 a.m. if I'm lucky, later if I'm not.

And then I wake up and do it all again.

🔒

This is the life I chose. This is the life I love. Even when it's hard, even when I'm tired, even when the words won't come—this is exactly where I want to be.

THE ROAD

KANSAS CITY, May 2—The visiting clubhouse at Kauffman Stadium is small and cramped. Lockers line the walls, filled with equipment and personal items. The players change quickly, eager to get out onto the field where there's space to breathe.

I find a corner and observe. Road trips have their own rhythm, different from home games. The players are away from their families, their routines disrupted. Some thrive on it. Others struggle.

🔒

The beat writers travel together, a small band of journalists who spend more time with each other than with anyone else in their lives. We share flights, share hotels, share meals, share complaints. We compete during the day and drink together at night.

It's a strange existence. You're always somewhere, but you're never really anywhere. Cities blur together—Kansas City looks like Cleveland looks like Minneapolis looks like Chicago. The only constant is the ballpark, the game, the story.

🔒

Tonight the Tigers lose, 6-2. The bats go cold, and the pitching can't compensate. It's one loss in a long season, but losses accumulate. They weigh on you, on the players, on everyone.

In the clubhouse afterward, Ausmus addresses the media. He's calm, measured, saying all the right things. We'll bounce back. We'll be fine. It's a long season. The words are true, but they feel hollow after a loss.

🔒

I file my story from the press box, watching the grounds crew prepare the field for tomorrow. The lights are still on, but the stands are empty. There's something melancholy about an empty stadium, all that space designed for crowds now silent and still.

I head back to the hotel. Tomorrow we fly to Minneapolis. Another city, another ballpark, another story to tell.

* * *

MINNEAPOLIS, May 5—Target Field is beautiful, one of the best ballparks in the league. The sight lines are perfect, the concourses are wide, and on a clear day you can see the downtown skyline beyond the outfield walls.

Today is not a clear day. It's raining, a steady drizzle that threatens to become something worse. The tarp is on the field, and everyone is waiting.

🔒

Rain delays are the worst part of the job. You sit and wait, not knowing if there will be a game or not. You can't leave because it might start. You can't write because there's nothing to write about. You just sit.

I use the time to catch up on emails, make phone calls, scroll through Twitter. The other writers do the same. We're all killing time together, separately.

After two hours, they call it. The game is postponed, rescheduled for tomorrow as part of a doubleheader. The players head back to the hotel. The writers head to the bar.

🔒

I order a beer and stare at the TV. SportsCenter is showing highlights from other games, other cities, other stories. Somewhere, baseball is being played. Just not here, not tonight.

Tomorrow there will be two games, two stories, twice the work. But for now, there's nothing to do but wait.

Waiting is part of the job too.

THE ALL-STAR BREAK

DETROIT, July 13—The first half of the season is over. The Tigers are 47-44, three games out of first place. Not great, not terrible. Good enough to have hope, not good enough to feel confident.

The All-Star break is a chance to breathe. Four days without games, without stories, without deadlines. The players scatter—some to the All-Star Game in Cincinnati, others to beaches and golf courses and whatever else ballplayers do when they're not playing ball.

🔒

I stay home. I sleep late, eat meals at normal times, see friends I haven't seen in months. I try to remember what life was like before baseball consumed it.

It's strange to have free time. I don't quite know what to do with myself. I find myself checking Twitter out of habit, looking for news that isn't there. The baseball world is quiet, and the quiet is unsettling.

🔒

By the third day, I'm restless. I miss the ballpark, the rhythm, the routine. I miss the players and the coaches and even the other writers. I miss having a purpose, a deadline, a reason to get out of bed.

This is what the job does to you. It becomes your identity, your reason for being. When it stops, even for a few days, you feel lost.

🔒

On the fourth day, I drive to Comerica Park just to look at it. The gates are locked, the parking lot empty. But the stadium is still there, waiting. In two days, the games will resume, and everything will start again.

I can't wait.

* * *

The second half begins with a thud. The Tigers lose their first three games, fall to six games back, and suddenly the optimism of spring feels like a distant memory.

The trade deadline looms. Dombrowski is working the phones, looking for reinforcements. Rumors swirl—this player might be available, that team might be selling. Nobody knows anything, but everybody has an opinion.

🔒

I chase every lead, make every call, write every story. The deadline is July 31st, and until then, anything is possible. The roster could look completely different in two weeks. Or it could look exactly the same.

That's the thing about baseball. You never know what's coming next. All you can do is show up, pay attention, and be ready when it happens.

I'm ready.
