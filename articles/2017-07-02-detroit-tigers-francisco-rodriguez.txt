The questions won&rsquo;t be answered in person anymore, but they still will come in text messages to Francisco Rodriguez&rsquo;s cell phone.

They will certainly come from Shane Greene and maybe Alex Wilson and Bruce Rondon, a few of the Tigers&rsquo; young relievers Rodriguez tried to mentor during his season and a half with the team. That time officially ended a week ago, when he was released by the Tigers after making no progress since losing the closer&rsquo;s role more than a month ago. It had been clear for quite some time that Rodriguez couldn&rsquo;t be counted on in key situations, and the team, starved for another option to support its struggling bullpen, gave him every chance to prove his worth.

But time has yet to expire on Rodriguez. So prideful of going out on his own terms and still fearless enough to find out if he can get the job done without dominant stuff,

What's lost are the tutorials for the young Tigers&rsquo; relievers, who saw up close how one of the best closers of all-time went about his business.

At age 35, most of Rodriguez&rsquo;s focus was molding them mentally, like Troy Percival and Mike Scioscia and others did with him as a youngster with the

&ldquo;He taught me how to slow the game down,&rdquo; Greene said. &ldquo;What to look for in hitters or how they take pitches or foul pitches off. He would help me dissect what was going on out there so I would know how the game was speeding up on me.&rdquo;

But above all, Greene said, Rodriguez taught him this: &ldquo;How to deal with failures and how to deal with success.&rdquo;

The end comes fast for closers, and it came fast for Rodriguez, who just last season saved 44 games with a 3.24 ERA and 1.13 WHIP. In two months this season, he went from the bullpen&rsquo;s stability to its liability. And so went another tale of a veteran Tigers closer, sent off into the sunset as a shell of his former self.

Rodriguez remembers the way this place would rock.

His entrance music would play, the reggaet&#243;n anthem that brought sold-out crowds to their feet, banging thunder sticks as a bean pole of a right-hander, all of 20 years old, would emerge from the bullpen.

&ldquo;The energy in this place,&rdquo; Rodriguez says, shaking his head, &ldquo;It was electrifying.&rdquo;

At this moment, he is K-Rod again. He is on the mound at Angel Stadium of Anaheim, this cruising comet of a righty reliever, captivating the baseball world with his fire fastball, crazy curveball and infectious flair.

&ldquo;You didn&rsquo;t even have time to think,&rdquo; he says. &ldquo;You didn&rsquo;t even feel it with your eyes. You were floating.&rdquo;

Rodriguez was untouchable on the biggest of stages, bolstering the back-end of the Angels bullpen as perhaps the biggest piece of their 2002 World Series championship team. He struck out 28 batters that postseason, earning the nickname K-Rod, even though he doesn&rsquo;t have a &lsquo;K&rsquo; in his name. It was the beginning of Hall of Fame-worthy numbers as a closer.

But on this May morning in 2017, sitting inside the stadium in Anaheim where his star was born, Rodriguez is no longer a closer. Not for the Tigers. He was removed from the role for the first time in his 14-year career days earlier, on May 9.

Rodriguez avoids talking about it, but he talks about other things for almost 20 minutes, about growing up in Venezuela and becoming baseball&rsquo;s original rock-star reliever.

He remembers the two-hour treks to practice, when he was 8 or 9 years old. He&rsquo;d take a bus down the mountains from his family&rsquo;s apartment in Caracas to the train station, then hop on the train and another bus. He played with Miguel Cabrera on the national team when he was 16 years old and after that summer signed with the Angels.

His first seasons in the organization were tough: He arrived in Montana for rookie ball knowing little English. His dad died while he was there. He was too small, the Angels thought, and never was going to hold up as a starting pitcher. They moved him to the bullpen.

That season, he was a late-September call-up, eligible for the postseason thanks to a disabled-list technicality that allowed teams to replace injured players. Rodriguez replaced Steve Green, who spent the entirety of the season on the 60-day DL.

When he made his major league debut on Sept. 18, 2002, Scioscia, the Angels&rsquo; manager, knew right away: &ldquo;If we make the playoffs, this guy has to be a part of it.&rdquo;

&ldquo;The first thing we saw about Frankie was obviously the incredible stuff he had but how mentally ready he was for the challenge of pitching in the big leagues,&rdquo; Scioscia said in May. &ldquo;He was well beyond his years as far as pose, as far as aggressiveness and the things you want to see in a baseball player, let alone a back-end reliever. This guy was special and you could see that his makeup was special.&rdquo;

His makeup was tested in Game 2 of the American League Division Series against the Yankees. Down 1-0 in the series, Scioscia called on Rodriguez, who had five September appearances under his belt, to protect a one-run in the bottom of the sixth inning at Yankee Stadium. He allowed a two-run home run to Alfonso Soriano instead.

&ldquo;I was devastated,&rdquo; Rodriguez said. &ldquo;I had the biggest opportunity of my life, and I let it go.&rdquo;

Rodriguez remembers Scioscia coming up to him while he sat at the end of the dugout. &ldquo;He says, &lsquo;It&rsquo;s your mess, you gotta clean it up.&rsquo; &rdquo;

Rodriguez remembers looking at the names due up on the Yankee Stadium scoreboard &mdash; Jason Giambi and Bernie Williams &mdash; and Scioscia telling him, &ldquo;You go out there and compete. I want you n there.&rdquo;

&ldquo;And after that,&rdquo; Rodriguez says, as if telling the story of K-Rod for the first time, &ldquo;I got so much confidence that I was unhittable.&rdquo;

He pitched a perfect seventh inning, the Angels rallied for the first of his five wins that postseason, and he learned an early closing lesson about bouncing back.

&ldquo;If he didn&rsquo;t let me go back out, I think I would have been done in that position,&rdquo; he says. &ldquo;My confidence would have been killed, and then after that, everything just went up.&rdquo;

Soon, Scioscia was leaning on him as his relief ace, pitching in high-leverage situations and for multiple innings, this kid coming out of nowhere to become the Angels&rsquo; most important man.

&ldquo;He became a rock star, instantly,&rdquo; Jose Mota, an Angels television broadcaster, said. &ldquo;It gives me chills. This crowd hadn&rsquo;t seen him &mdash; they were following everything on television &mdash; and the buildup of Francisco Rodriguez and then, man, when he would take the mound here, it was wild.&rdquo;

Many years later &mdash; after stints with the Mets, Brewers and Orioles &mdash; Rodriguez can still see it. He talks about the noise and the adrenaline and about that time he struck out Jeff Kent in the World Series.

&ldquo;It&rsquo;s hard to describe, really,&rdquo; he says. &ldquo;You didn&rsquo;t even have time to think. You didn&rsquo;t even feel it with your eyes. You were floating. It was so special. It seems like last year or two years ago.&rdquo;

And then, after a brief pause, he says: &ldquo;This game can make you forget a lot of things.&rdquo;

Rodriguez ranks fourth all-time in saves with 437. He is of the best closers of this century, saving over 40 games in six seasons and holding a tough record to beat with 62 saves in 2008. He was really good for a really long time.

But in Detroit, veteran closers aren&rsquo;t remembered for their really good years. They&rsquo;re remembered for another blown save and getting booed at home and bristling with reporters. It was never going to end well, not with the pitches Rodriguez was throwing this year, and predictably, it did not. The lasting image the locals will have of the once-great K-Rod is him walking off the mound after back-to-back blown saves against the Athletics in early May, just before losing his job as closer.

Often times this season, Greene and other relievers would crowd around Rodriguez&rsquo;s locker after games, analyzing their outings. Last season, it was Rondon, who, in a fitting twist of irony, took Rodriguez&rsquo;s spot on the 25-man roster.

They won&rsquo;t see Rodriguez every day anymore, but they&rsquo;ll be hearing from him. Greene received a few words of encouragement from him after a loss in San Diego last week.

&ldquo;It&rsquo;s different without him here,&rdquo; Greene said, &ldquo;But I think Frankie&rsquo;s presence will still be around one way or another.&rdquo;
