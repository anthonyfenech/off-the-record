HEADLINE: Sometimes the hardest part of Mother's Day is letting go
BYLINE: Shawn Windsor
DATE: 2019-05-12
URL: https://www.freep.com/story/sports/columnists/shawn-windsor/2019/05/12/mothers-day-shawn-windsor/1177163001/
---

My mother grew up without a toilet in the house. That isn&rsquo;t to romanticize her modest upbringing. It&rsquo;s just the way things were.

A blue-collar father. A stay-at-home mom. A small town in northern Indiana.

When she returned as an adult, often with me and my younger brother in tow, we&rsquo;d walk from my grandfather&rsquo;s house to the only main street and watch the tractor-pull competition, then head to the drugstore for a cherry cola.

I struggled to imagine my mother &mdash; Elizabeth Burnside &mdash; in her childhood home. To square the towering, worldly figure in my day-to-day life with the place that formed her.

By the time I was a teenager, she&rsquo;d lived &mdash; and had traveled &mdash; all over the world. She spoke five languages &mdash; French, German, Japanese, Spanish and Portuguese &mdash; and dabbled in a bit of Russian. And her cooking borrowed from just as many cuisines.

Many of those recipes are tucked inside a metal recipe box that&rsquo;s stored in a shed in my backyard. On occasion, I think about finding that box and trying to recreate her shrimp curry or egg rolls or manicotti.

But then I freeze. Because I&rsquo;m still not ready. Because while the taste and smells will evoke memories from long ago, it won&rsquo;t quite taste or smell the same. And I don&rsquo;t want to give up the memory of those dishes coming from her hands.

What am I waiting for?

That&rsquo;s a question I grapple with these days. And it seems like an important question on Mother&rsquo;s Day.

Whatever else you think about Mother&rsquo;s Day, whether you find it too commercialized or too cloying, the holiday arose from a desire for unity during a time of war, and officially hit our calendar in 1914.

For those whose mothers are still around, it&rsquo;s a day to show appreciation and acknowledge the sacrifice and complexity of motherhood, in ways large and small. It&rsquo;s also a reminder that we don&rsquo;t need a holiday to honor the person who birthed us.

For those whose mothers are gone, it&rsquo;s a day to reflect.

This year I&rsquo;m thinking of my mother more on Mother&rsquo;s Day than I have in the past. Mostly because I no longer think about her every day.

It&rsquo;s not a conscious choice. It&rsquo;s simply time. And grief fades with time.

I miss the grief in a way. It kept me close to her, painful as it was.

She&rsquo;d had frontal lobe dementia (FTD), a particularly sinister disease in which the front portion of the brain shrinks. FTD strips away your ability to process information. It acts like a digital virus, slowing the system it infiltrates to a crawl.

In the beginning, you can&rsquo;t recall words. Or ideas. Eventually, you lose the critical-thinking apparatus that separates an adult brain from a child&rsquo;s.

As heartbreaking as it is to watch, there is a stretch where the filter between your brain and your tongue disappears, and suddenly you&rsquo;ve got a mother who will say anything, to anyone, at any time.

This can be refreshing, of course, like when a pedestrian steps into a crosswalk when the light is green, and forces you to stop.

&ldquo;Hey (expletive),&rdquo; my mother would yell, then grin. But then the dark humor fades, too, as the words began to cease coming at all.

My mother&rsquo;s been gone for three and a half years, but she&rsquo;s really been gone for almost a decade &mdash; she was diagnosed five years before she died.

It's made mourning her tricky. In the days and weeks after she passed, a rush of memories from my childhood came back, and I felt her presence in a manner I hadn&rsquo;t in years.

After a while, though, maybe by that holiday season, I struggled to remember her beyond her diminished state. In a sense, I&rsquo;d lost her again.

The effect was mildly disorienting. Like the trips I used to take to her childhood home, when I&rsquo;d try to make sense of who she was and where I fit into her life.

To regain my equilibrium, I went out searching for her. Both on my own &mdash; reconnecting with parts of her family I hadn&rsquo;t seen in decades &mdash; and through my job, making time on football or basketball or baseball road trips to seek out folks who meant something to my mother.

Like her, I&rsquo;m fortunate enough to get paid to travel. And, like her, I try to follow a rule she taught me when I was young &mdash; if someone you love is within a four-hour drive of your destination, make the drive.

And, so, I do. As often as I can.

Meeting friends of hers I knew as &ldquo;aunt&rdquo; or &ldquo;uncle&rdquo; as a child for lunch. Seeking out the living rooms and kitchen tables of those she met and loved during her travels.

Last year, I sat on a couch in Amsterdam &mdash; not a work trip &mdash; in a living room dotted with a few of my mother&rsquo;s artifacts and photos. And while I&rsquo;ve always known there are pieces of her past spread in homes around the world, seeing them up close took me back to a time when she was the center of my world.

I&rsquo;ve slowed down in my search the last six months or so, as I began to realize that what I&rsquo;d been looking for was best found at home, inside me. For she left plenty of herself in there.

After her death, I just couldn&rsquo;t find it.

All it took was time.

To understand that even as she spent much of her life flying through the clouds seeking out the world, the most important trips she took were back to a little town in Indiana &mldr; hauling me and my brother, because my mother didn&rsquo;t want to forget who she was.

I understand this now, and that she and I were searching for the same things.

For the first time in my life, I know what I have to do on Mother&rsquo;s Day: Tell her I love her. Then let her go.

She&rsquo;s been here all along.
