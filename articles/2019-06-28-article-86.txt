OMAHA, Neb. &mdash; Their final meeting began a minute early, at 3:14 p.m. Central on Wednesday afternoon, when a Michigan baseball staffer closed the door to the Dodge Ballroom on the second floor of the downtown Omaha Marriott, exactly an hour and 47 minutes earlier than their team meetings throughout the season.

That time, 5:01, had a purpose &mdash; attention to detail &mdash; and was the last Bakich-ism on the back of the Wolverines&rsquo; blue workout shirts, at the bottom of an inverted pyramid, right below &ldquo;LFG.&rdquo;

They walked in a single file, standing out in their uniforms among the commoners, who were ambling about while attending various work conferences on the same floor, around a table where a medical academic named Brian sat, writing notes for an upcoming presentation.

&ldquo;Good luck, fellas,&rdquo; he said. &ldquo;You got it.&rdquo;

Brian had noted the Wolverines a day earlier from the adjacent conference room his company occupied, noticed their confidence and swagger &mdash; they won Game 1 of the College World Series finals on Monday, loud and high-fiving on their way out &mdash; but on Wednesday, with the series now tied and a winner-take-all game just hours away, he sensed a change just by the way they passed by.

&ldquo;They were so loose yesterday,&rdquo; he said. &ldquo;I see them walking today, they still have the swagger, but they look tight. They were chill yesterday, but they seem tense, tight.&rdquo;

&ldquo;We&rsquo;ll see,&rdquo; Brian said. &ldquo;I think they got it.&rdquo;

On the table in front of him was a book: &ldquo;Winning Teams, Winning Cultures.&rdquo;

Michigan baseball has fielded winning teams in each of Bakich&rsquo;s seven seasons in Ann Arbor &mdash; a Big Ten tournament title, a couple NCAA tournament appearances &mdash; but this winning culture, the one where a motor coach carrying them is cheered on by hundreds of fans outside the team hotel at the College World Series, was created quickly.

Flanked by police motorcycles, the team bus departed the hotel at 3:29 p.m., half a mile away from TD Ameritrade Park, where they arrived ready to win a national championship for a second consecutive day.

The tension Brian sensed was not evident inside the Wolverines&rsquo; dugout during batting practice, and when Paige &mdash;

&ldquo;I&rsquo;m sorry I took your spot,&rdquo; he told Nwogu, U-M&rsquo;s leadoff hitter and DH.

Nwogu strained his left quadriceps the night before, pulling up lame a few steps before first base, helped off the field, and insisted the crutches were not for show.

&ldquo;I&rsquo;m going yard today,&rdquo; Paige said.

He was batting ninth, the most obvious of decoys, a gamesmanship move that Bakich picked up from years of coaching under Vanderbilt head coach Tim Corbin.

After four swings in the batting cages, Paige declared himself retired from hitting, and was pinch-hit for by sophomore infielder Riley Bertram in the top of the second inning. At the time, the Wolverines were riding high. They led, 1-0, after a flurry of singles to open the game, only furthering their parents&rsquo; belief from the past two weeks that it was not &ldquo;if,&rdquo; but &ldquo;when&rdquo; this team of destiny won it all.

But after Jordan Brewer&rsquo;s RBI single &mdash; U-M&rsquo;s third straight hit against Vanderbilt righty Mason Hickman &mdash; they mustered nothing more in the inning, leaving runners on first and second base with three consecutive strikeouts.

A three-run deficit turned into four, then six, and as Kauffmann greeted sophomore righty Jeff Criswell on the front steps of the dugout in the innings that followed, the Wolverines&rsquo; offense could not catch up, going down quietly as their national championship dreams were dashed.

There were long embraces and tears, Bakich dodging stage equipment to share a moment with Corbin, and a line of handshakes while smoke from fireworks filled the stadium.

U-M watched it all &mdash; the dogpile, celebration and trophy presentation &mdash; partly because Bakich wanted them to, but also because they didn&rsquo;t want the ride to end.

They weren&rsquo;t supposed to be here, everyone said, but they didn&rsquo;t believe it. They were the underdogs and wore it proudly, the team from the north that could, playing with house money, always content with one more game together.

And when there were no games left, senior Jack Bredeson wandered aimlessly with watery eyes, junior Tommy Henry hung over the dugout railing, just staring, and sophomore Jack Blomgren tried his best to ignore his family, who stood above the dugout with a giant Fathead and a sign that read, &ldquo;WE LOVE JACK!&rdquo;

His family made hearts with their hands and chanted, &ldquo;We&rsquo;ll be back!&rdquo; and finally got his attention. They would see him back at the hotel. &ldquo;I love you,&rdquo; his mom said.

The Wolverines left the ballpark while Bakich finished his fifth interview of the night, sneaking behind a pack of reporters and back onto the bus.

Inside the clubhouse, Henry fought through tears during an interview with the Big Ten Network and couldn&rsquo;t fathom taking his uniform off.

&ldquo;I might sleep in it tonight and wear it until they make me turn it in,&rdquo; he said. &ldquo;These last three years have been a dream come true.&rdquo;

He would spend the next few minutes breathing deeply, trying to hold it back.

Kerr felt nauseous taking his uniform off. Kauffmann went through the media wringer. Bredeson tried to ease the pain by flipping through his phone.

&ldquo;The feeling is crushed,&rdquo; Henry said. &ldquo;Just because when we walked off the field, we were wanting to be in that dogpile. We fought as hard as we could, we gave it everything we had, and I&rsquo;m proud of everyone in this room.&rdquo;

But nobody was prouder than Bakich, who, after years of trying to will his talented teams to victory, allowed them to take the lead late in the season &mdash; for better or worse &mdash; and watched them respond with a program-altering month. It was a month that will be felt for years to come, perhaps putting U-M back on the college baseball map the way they were 35 years ago when the school last reached the College World Series, the way he envisioned when taking the job seven years ago.

&ldquo;There&rsquo;s just so many story lines that have come out of this group getting knocked down and getting back up and fighting through adversity and just having a bunch of good dudes,&rdquo; Bakich said. &ldquo;And I think that future teams will always reference back to this particular group as a model of consistency, of how to take care of the littlest of things and having total buy-in, never doubting, even when it would be very easy to doubt, even when we had some slip-ups.

&ldquo;But they didn&rsquo;t, and they stuck with it. They stayed positive and they got hot when they needed to, and they made the run that we needed to in order to put ourselves in position to be one game away from the national championship.&rdquo;

The bus pulled up to the hotel at 10:52 p.m., the players walking in to cheers eight minutes later, &ldquo;Let&rsquo;s Go Blue!&rdquo; chants, hugs and yes, more tears.

Kauffmann&rsquo;s father, Ray, found Fetter almost immediately and pulled him in.

&ldquo;The guy who does so much for the kid,&rdquo; Ray said. &ldquo;He put him on a path. They talk about family, caring, and they walk the walk. It&rsquo;s been an incredible three years. It&rsquo;s tough to say goodbye.&rdquo;

But in a way, it wasn&rsquo;t a goodbye, rather a "see you later." Team 153 did something to the U-M community, something seismic, something that brought together teams of the past and planted seeds of the future.

&ldquo;You could look at it just at face value and say this is the best team that Michigan baseball has produced,&rdquo; Bakich said. &ldquo;But in the modern era, to do what we did this year is extremely special and I think bodes very well for the future of our program.&rdquo;

Six members of the school&rsquo;s last national championship team in 1962 &mdash; including John Kerr, Jimmy&rsquo;s grandfather &mdash; were around. As were more than 20 members &mdash; including Derek Kerr, Jimmy&rsquo;s father &mdash; of the 1984 team, the last to reach Omaha. A handful from Fetter&rsquo;s squad in 2007 that upended top-ranked Vanderbilt in the super regional.

&ldquo;These guys don&rsquo;t even know what they just did for the University of Michigan and the baseball program,&rdquo; Derek said. &ldquo;They will know eventually, but they don&rsquo;t know right now.

&ldquo;This has brought the program back where it needs to be and I think it&rsquo;s going to stay there for a long time.&rdquo;

The Wolverines don&rsquo;t understand it yet, not in the agony of defeat, on the doorstep of doing something deemed impossible &mdash; for U-M, the Big Ten, or any team that plays most of their season in bad weather.

&ldquo;It was amazing,&rdquo; Derek said. &ldquo;I&rsquo;m so proud of every one of these kids.&rdquo;

The Wolverines didn&rsquo;t win it all, but they played as many games as they possibly could have together, leaving a mark on the U-M record book like so many &mdash; passing on more accomplished programs &mdash; set out to do.

&ldquo;There&rsquo;s a lot of people who counted on all of us, but we knew if everyone in here believed and believed in each other, that good things can happen,&rdquo; Henry said. &ldquo;We came up a little it short, but once the pain goes away, we&rsquo;ll reflect back, and we&rsquo;ll be proud of this season.&rdquo;

It would begin to go away sooner than they expected. Kauffmann put on a cowboy hat, the one he bought at that Kenny Chesney concert before the team took off. Brewer, 22, held not one, but two beers. &ldquo;Hail To The Victors&rdquo; came on at closing time of the Irish pub next door.

But first, they went upstairs, back to the Dodge Ballroom, past the real estate conference that had long since closed up shop, around the table Brian sat at, and Team 153 &mdash; perhaps the most influential team in Michigan baseball history &mdash; shared one last meal together.
