Here, a hundred or so miles away from Gardenhire&rsquo;s current spring training address in Lakeland, Fla., some of the people who know him best weigh in on the man who will make his debut in the Old English D

On the backfields, after hitting groundball after groundball to a third base prospect, an older man is asked if he knows Ron Gardenhire.

He is Sam Perlozzo, the Twins&rsquo; infield and baserunning instructor, who was Gardenhire&rsquo;s manager with the Triple-A Tidewater Mets in 1986.

&ldquo;He&rsquo;s a good man,&rdquo; Perlozzo said. &ldquo;Gardy&rsquo;s been a good man ever since I&rsquo;ve known him. Doesn&rsquo;t forget you. Always got time for you. He turned into a darn good baseball guy. He&rsquo;s got his own ideas and sticks with them. He&rsquo;s got a commitment to the game and to himself and I think it showed with all his years managing in Minnesota.&rdquo;

Gardenhire played with Perlozzo at the tail end of his playing career. In 1986, Tidewater won the Triple-A World Series. Perlozzo remembers Gardenhire as an energetic player: &ldquo;Half the time I thought we could have just kept him in an ice packet. I think everything with his body, that&rsquo;s how hard he played.&rdquo;

It was hard to forecast Gardenhire&rsquo;s managing future back then, Perlozzo said, but it&rsquo;s not a surprise to him, not after keeping up with him over the years and working with him briefly in Minnesota.

&ldquo;He was a headsy guy,&rdquo; Perlozzo said. &ldquo;Always thinking. If you asked him a question, he always had a good answer for you. But at the same time, he worked at it. He had certain things that he would do that he believes in and he still does it. He still does them. He didn&rsquo;t just up and leave Minnesota and just go over to Detroit and not do some of the things he does all the time. That&rsquo;s the stuff I liked about him.&rdquo;

On a field next to Perlozzo, there's a familiar name. Gardenhire&rsquo;s son, Toby is coaching third base and managing today&rsquo;s minor-league game for Class A Cedar Rapids. After coaching in college for five seasons, Toby Gardenhire has followed his dad&rsquo;s footsteps into the professional ranks, about to embark on his first season as a manager in the Twins system.

Asked what his dad is all about, Toby Gardenhire said, &ldquo;He&rsquo;s pretty straightforward with everything he does. He&rsquo;s good with the young guys, with young players and kind of the way he&rsquo;s a happy-go-lucky guy. He is exactly what you see. So that&rsquo;s, to me, that&rsquo;s been his biggest strength. He is exactly what you think he is, exactly what you see. He doesn&rsquo;t hold anything back. He&rsquo;s the same person, day in and day out, no matter whether he&rsquo;s at the ballpark, whether he&rsquo;s out eating dinner, wherever he is, that&rsquo;s him.&rdquo;

He describes a person who talks to anybody &mdash; players, coaches, fans, media, grounds crew, police officers.

&ldquo;That&rsquo;s one of those things, special people do that,&rdquo; Toby Gardenhire said. &ldquo;And he&rsquo;s a special person.&rdquo;

This was the case in Tiger Town this spring: Gardenhire signing autographs for every last fan after the Tigers&rsquo; early workouts. Gardenhire catching the first pitch from a number of war veterans before games.

A similar scene is recalled by Twins play-by-play broadcaster Dick Bremer, who counts this as his favorite Gardenhire story.

&ldquo;My most vivid memory of his time with the Twins &mdash;when he was a third base coach, with Tom Kelly&rsquo;s approval &mdash; on Sunday mornings, he&rsquo;d have the coaches&rsquo; kids and some of the other little kids around, boys and girls, and he&rsquo;d pitch batting practice to them on the field at the Metrodome. And so I asked him whether my son could be a part of it and he said, absolutely and all that.

&ldquo;Then, he got the job as the manager and I thought those days were done. But there he was, as a big league manager, still down on a knee with a bucket of balls, under-handing these balls to little kids, who &mdash; I know for my son and probably everybody else who did it &mdash; will never forget the experience of taking batting practice on Sunday mornings at a big league ballpark against a Major League manager.&rdquo;

But the best insight came from inside Hammond Stadium, where two of Gardenhire&rsquo;s former players &mdash; All-Star Brian Dozier and former All-Star Justin Morneau &mdash; summed him up in a similar way.

Dozier: &ldquo;&ldquo;You hear the whole clich&#233; saying a player&rsquo;s manager, and that&rsquo;s just thrown out there very loosely, but he is definitely a player&rsquo;s manager.&rdquo;

Morneau: &ldquo;Player&rsquo;s manager. &mldr; For sure. But he was also aware of the line between manager and player; there was always some good division there.&rdquo;

Both Dozier and Morneau credit Gardenhire with developing them as young players, which will be his foremost task with this rebuilding Tigers team. For Dozier, it was working with him as he struggled, helping him transition from shortstop to second base, and providing him the discipline needed to take the next step.

&ldquo;He definitely holds you accountable,&rdquo; Dozier said. &ldquo;That&rsquo;s one thing that I was brought up, where my dad demanded you do the right things and he held me accountable for when I wasn&rsquo;t doing those things. And the exact same with Gardy. Whether you like it or not, he kind of told you what he really thought and I think people around the game really respect more than ever the manager or person that&rsquo;s candid with you. And he&rsquo;s definitely that.&rdquo;

With Morneau, Gardenhire was the person to finally hammer home how much potential he had. In one notable instance, Morneau was having too much fun with his friends on a trip to Seattle. Gardenhire and former general manager Terry Ryan called Morneau into his office the next day. The message: You are throwing your talent away.

&ldquo;I thought I was getting sent down,&rdquo; Morneau said. &ldquo;I just remember as a young player how comfortable he made me feel and how we put in a lot of work together. Defensive stuff. And I don&rsquo;t know if it was because he was fighting to keep me on the team because they always said that defense was the one thing that was keeping me out of the big leagues, but he put in the work with me. I don&rsquo;t know how much time managers have for that stuff anymore, but we would come out in spring training, we would come out early and we&rsquo;d do hands drills and different stuff. He was always there for me.&rdquo;

Together, the Twins people polled have plenty of stories &mdash; many not fit for print &mdash; and speak of a manager whose pranks and humor plays well during the grind of a six-month season. He is known to have a pen that delivers an electric shock on his desk. He also approaches his job like a teacher would: Like students, every player is different and needs to be taught a certain way. But perhaps his biggest lesson &mdash; one he continues to manage by &mdash; came during his playing career as a utility player, or, as he likes to say, &ldquo;a futility player.&rdquo;

&ldquo;I always say, one of Gardy&rsquo;s greatest strengths was he never forgot how difficult it was to play baseball,&rdquo; Morneau said. &ldquo;For a young player, that&rsquo;s hard to grasp. With a young team over there, I think they&rsquo;re going to love him.&rdquo;
