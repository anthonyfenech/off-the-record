&ldquo;It&rsquo;s clear to anyone that follows Tigers baseball that our organization is undergoing a significant transformation,&rdquo; Ilitch said in a statement released by the team. &ldquo;I&rsquo;ve been impressed with Al&rsquo;s leadership and focus, and the steadfast way he has led our baseball operations since becoming general manager.

Avila, 60, is 263-360 in nearly four full years on the job, since replacing Dave Dombrowski, on Aug. 4, 2016. Dombrowski is now the Red Sox president and general manager.

In Avila's three full seasons, the Tigers &mdash; who began a much-needed rebuilding process in 2017 by trading Justin Verlander to the Houston Astros &mdash; have finished 2 &#189; games out of an AL wild-card spot (2016), dead last in baseball (2017) and with the fifth-worst record in the majors (2018). This season, they are on pace to lose more than 100 games.

&ldquo;We&rsquo;re right in the middle of it,&rdquo; said Avila of the rebuilding process. &ldquo;It&rsquo;s a tough time. You start it, you start moving along, there&rsquo;s a fast pace to it and at the end, when really, quite frankly, you start seeing it come together and then you can see the light.

&ldquo;And then, in the middle, it&rsquo;s the darkest hour. That&rsquo;s what we&rsquo;re going through right now. There&rsquo;s a little light in different areas and that&rsquo;s what we&rsquo;re clinging to.&rdquo;

Ilitch was not present for Avila&rsquo;s press conference. Avila did not disclose the length of his extension. He was previously signed to a five-year deal, through the 2020 season.

Under Avila&rsquo;s watch, the Tigers have lost a lot of games.

In 2016, Avila inherited a win-now team with a bloated payroll, a barren farm system and a front office far behind the times in analytics. Under his watch, the bulk of the payroll has been pared down, the farm system replenished through trades and drafts, and the analytics staff has more than tripled.

But those improvements alone don't guarantee success: As front offices continue to adapt, especially in their analysis of veteran value, teams have shied away from trading top prospects right when the Tigers are trying to acquire them.

They will be a player on the trade market once again this month, with left-handed starter Matthew Boyd, right fielder Nicholas Castellanos, closer Shane Greene and righty reliever Joe Jimenez all considered to be on the trade block.

&ldquo;We&rsquo;re not unique to this situation or process,&rdquo; Avila said. &ldquo;What we have to do is have the courage to see it through, and every day I show up to work, I gotta face that every day. So that&rsquo;s what we&rsquo;re doing, and I think we&rsquo;re making some big strides here.&rdquo;

Those strides will not be evident for years.

But the foundation Avila has built was enough for Ilitch, who first broached the topic of a contract extension in the spring.

&ldquo;At this point, I think Chris just felt, &lsquo;Hey, let&rsquo;s get you done,&rsquo;&rdquo; Avila said. &ldquo;Let&rsquo;s get you out of the way so we can focus on the task at hand, which is the trading deadline.&rdquo;

The Tigers had no real options: In Year 3 of rebuilding &mdash;despite the expected lack of major league success &mdash; it would have been detrimental to shift gears halfway through.

The Tigers needed a full-scale rebuild. This is what that looks like. We've known the plan for a while. It's the execution &mdash; drafting, developing and signing at a championship level &mdash; that matters, that good teams succeed at and the others struggle with. We won't see the success of that for years.

&ldquo;I&rsquo;d rather be on the way to win 90 games at the big league level, but this is something we have to go through,&rdquo; Avila said. &ldquo;That&rsquo;s what rebuilding is.&rdquo;

Avila knows the scrutiny of his position and also the frustration of Tigers fans, still jaded from the contending years, who are having a tough time swallowing the pill of another last-place season.

&ldquo;People come up to me all the time,&rdquo; Avila said. &ldquo;&rsquo;Man, I really feel for you.&rsquo; &lsquo;I wouldn&rsquo;t want your job in 100 years.&rsquo; I really get a lot of that. Baseball people understand how tough that is, because you have to put up with all the scrutiny.&rdquo;

But sometimes &mdash; like the man who stopped him in downtown Birmingham over the winter &mdash; the messages are confident.

&ldquo;I know it&rsquo;s hard, but we trust you,&rdquo; the man said. &ldquo;We know you guys will get it done.&rdquo;

In four years, Avila has yet to earn everyone&rsquo;s trust. Such is the territory of rebuilding, where losses show up well before prized prospects do.

&ldquo;This is even worse,&rdquo; he said. &ldquo;You have to have courage, you have to have conviction, you have to have a tremendous staff. We plan to be successful, but the only way we&rsquo;re going to be successful is if my entire staff does a knock-up job.&rdquo;

They can see that path to success, but still so far away.

The losses &mdash; especially this year&rsquo;s &mdash; have piled up, making patience and trust difficult. But on Friday afternoon, Avila stood in front of reporters and spoke about receiving a contract extension.

He has earned his boss&rsquo; trust. The plan remains intact. It won&rsquo;t be painless &ndash; they never are &mdash; and it might not work.

But given the progress made, the contract extension was merely confirmation of was expected: Avila is the Tigers' guy to see the process through.
