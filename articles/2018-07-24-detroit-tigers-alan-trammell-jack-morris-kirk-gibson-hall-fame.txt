Few know Alan Trammell and Jack Morris better than Kirk Gibson.

Gibson, teammates with the two for a combined 12 major league seasons, saw both players ascend to Hall of Fame status with the Tigers.

Their friendships &mdash; borne through competition and winning, on-field success and off-field debauchery &mdash; have grown as the years have passed. With Trammell and Morris set to be inducted into the National Baseball Hall of Fame this weekend in Cooperstown, N.Y., Gibson shared, in his own words, the stories of their friendships.

&ldquo;I met Tram in 1978 when I was getting ready to get drafted. We had kind of privately worked a deal with the Tigers &mdash; everybody was asking me if I&rsquo;d play baseball and I said nope,

&ldquo;You know Tram, he&rsquo;s never really been a jerk where he&rsquo;d be mean to a guy. He&rsquo;d want to get over there and see who the guy is &mdash; me &mdash; and then there&rsquo;s some people making some comments at me like, 'Throw him a curveball,' or a changeup or give him a real bat. 'You need an aluminum bat?' I was cranking them. And then I got drafted by the Tigers, and then after that it was spring training, we all got together. I met him, Jack (Morris), Rosey (Dave Rozema), Peaches (Dan Petry).

&ldquo;Tram, the way he is now, he&rsquo;s got a good reputation

"And I&rsquo;ve always said this:

&ldquo;He&rsquo;s very, very athletic. Would you believe that? I mean, ridiculous. Not flashy. He wasn&rsquo;t as physical, big. He ran good, he had good technique. Smart. His arm wasn&rsquo;t great but he was accurate going right, going left, coming in, going back. Tram just has a sense. He knows where to move. Good hands. Tram was a natural. The other thing he had was he had desire. He had great desire."

&ldquo;Mid-'80s, we got snowed out Opening Day, got on a plane, went to Kansas City, had a workout, went out in Kansas City. Me, Tram, Rosey for sure. I don&rsquo;t know if Jack was there or not. But I had my wine glass sitting there, OK, and Rosey goes to hit Tram in the back of the head, and he ducks right into my glass. Forty stitches. Rosey&rsquo;s like, 'Oh, no.' My glass hit it. He looked up to me, and it was just like, 'Whomp!' He ducked really quick into my glass because of Rosey.

We were already on kind of double, triple, quadruple, gazillion probation, OK. And he had 40 stitches, and we went to the park. Sparky, he was about ready to be done with us all. It was Opening Day. So sure enough, he got sewed up and it was big. He&rsquo;s got a huge scar. Forty stitches. I mean huge. So he came the next day and had a little beanie cap on; it was funny. We were secretly trying to figure out how we could cut the foam out of the hat so he could play. Sparky, he ripped us. He got into us, man. He said, 'I know it&rsquo;s you and you and you!' We took all the blame.

&ldquo;Jack was a pitcher and so it developed. Remember, Jack, he was from BYU, he was out there. Our personalities were very similar. We got along; we bonded real quick. With Jack, my initial bonding with him was just communication because we&rsquo;d go out and the first thing we&rsquo;d do is run a mile around Joker Marchant Stadium, around the outside. And Jack, he was so damn competitive, just like Tram, so a jog around the Marchant Stadium around the inside of the track, four laps around, was a race to me.

"So we&rsquo;d hook up, and I&rsquo;d run, and I had a great kick, and they&rsquo;d have guys try and tire me out, and then he&rsquo;d come out and try and he couldn&rsquo;t beat me. I&rsquo;d be so sore sometimes. I&rsquo;d say, 'Screw it,' my calves would be so sore, I&rsquo;m just going to let those guys win. Two laps around, I just couldn&rsquo;t let Jack beat me. He&rsquo;s so damn competitive, and he&rsquo;ll tell you. I don&rsquo;t think he ever &mdash; I know he didn&rsquo;t beat me. Nobody ever beat me in that

&ldquo;We came from totally different places. I came from football; he came from where he came from out West. He was a pitcher; I was a position player. Jack, he&rsquo;d rub it in our faces. He&rsquo;d be walking out, it&rsquo;d be 9 a.m. and we&rsquo;d just be ready to go out. He&rsquo;d be walking out, 'Oh, I&rsquo;m going fishing.' 'Oh, I&rsquo;m going hog hunting today.' He&rsquo;d rub it in. He always rubbed (crap) in. That&rsquo;s who he was. He wanted to mentally punish you.

&ldquo;He was an animal on the mound.

"But you know, regardless of it all, Jack was there to answer the bell. Like, unbelievable. You could really feel that Jack didn&rsquo;t care how many runs he gave up, he just pitched to win the damn game. His desire to win was unbelievable. He&rsquo;d come in after giving up six runs in the first inning, and we&rsquo;d all be sitting there because he&rsquo;s pouting around. He&rsquo;d come in and we&rsquo;d be going, OK. And he&rsquo;d say, 'Well, I&rsquo;ve never lost with nine! Give me some damn runs!' He&rsquo;d just break the ice. He&rsquo;d be pouting on the mound and Darrell Evans would go over there and he&rsquo;d go, 'Wah!' He&rsquo;d be pouting, and we&rsquo;d all be yelling at him, 'Wah!' But it went both ways. This guy is unmerciful. He&rsquo;d do the same thing, you know. We&rsquo;d scream at him; he&rsquo;d scream at us."

&ldquo;I remember one time Sparky came to take him out and Jack went to hand him the ball and dropped it. Jack went, 'Oops' and picked up the ball. He had a meeting and Sparky said, 'Nobody shows me up. I won&rsquo;t show you up.' He said, 'You don&rsquo;t say anything to the press about me and I won&rsquo;t say anything about you. Nobody throws anybody under the bus.' "
