PHILADELPHIA &mdash; Harold Castro had already taken early batting practice and was hanging out when Triple-A Toledo manager Doug Mientkiewicz pulled him into his office at Fifth Third Field around 3 p.m. on Tuesday afternoon.

&ldquo;You&rsquo;re going to the big leagues,&rdquo; Mientkiewicz said. &ldquo;And you&rsquo;re going to fly at 6 (p.m.), so you better hurry up.&rdquo;

Thus began the craziest day of

&ldquo;It was really crazy,&rdquo; Castro said.

After Mientkiewicz told him that he was called up to the Tigers &ndash; who were playing in Philadelphia at 7:10 p.m. &mdash; he called his dad, hustled to pack what he needed, and left the ballpark.

&ldquo;Pack enough luggage for what you need for just two days,&rdquo; Mientkiewicz told him. &ldquo;Then you can come back and get the rest.&rdquo;

Castro made a quick stop at his downtown Toledo apartment and met with his dad, who then drove him 45 minutes north to Metro Airport. He arrived at the McNamara Terminal at 4:45 p.m., in plenty of time to make his 6 p.m. flight to Philadelphia.

Tigers director of team travel Brian Britten laughed off the notion that Castro&rsquo;s circumstances were any different than the many roster moves the team will make over the course of the season: &ldquo;They tell me who&rsquo;s coming up and I book the flight,&rdquo; he said.

There have been plenty more challenging situations, like the time years ago when Ryan Raburn was with the Mud Hens, who were playing in Durham, N.C., and spent an off-day visiting family in Florida. That day, the Tigers needed Raburn in the big leagues.

He took a flight out of Tampa. Easy enough. But his bags remained in Durham, and the Tigers bought a plane ticket just for Raburn&rsquo;s bags. The logistics company DHL dropped the bags off, picked them up at the airport, and took them to the stadium.

&ldquo;This one was definitely one of those weird ones,&rdquo; manager Ron Gardenhire said.

The Tigers didn&rsquo;t make the move official until they knew Castro had boarded his flight. If he didn&rsquo;t, they had two decisions: Use Harrison as an emergency bench player or place him on the IL anyways, playing a man short, so they could get him back a day earlier, once he&rsquo;s healthy.

&ldquo;After we made the decision at 6 p.m. to DL him, I put him on my roster once they told me he was on the plane,&rdquo; Gardenhire said. &ldquo;If they hadn&rsquo;t told me he was on the plane, I wouldn&rsquo;t have put him on the roster.&rdquo;

Castro arrived to the ballpark in the fourth inning.

He had a road uniform hanging in his locker because clubhouse manager Jim Schmakel had a uniform made up for him in short order. Schmakel travels with a uniform of every player on the 40-man roster, but Castro was not on the 40-man roster. Schmakel also travels with blank jerseys, letters and numbers. Often times, the team will send uniforms over to the home team for them to stitch together.

Castro got dressed, stretched inside the clubhouse and made it into the dugout by the fifth inning. He took a round of batting practice in the cage shortly thereafter, and then another in the seventh inning, before his name was called to pinch-hit in the ninth.

&ldquo;You gotta be ready for it,&rdquo; he said. &ldquo;I wasn&rsquo;t thinking I was going to play because I got here in the third inning, but I was ready for it. I came in the fifth inning to hit and prepare for the time they need me.&rdquo;

In the span of seven hours, Castro went from the minor leagues to the major leagues, Toledo to Detroit to Philadelphia, and had one heck of a story to tell his grandkids one day.

&ldquo;Sure, it&rsquo;s a long day,&rdquo; Gardenhire said, &ldquo;Welcome back to the big leagues.&rdquo;
