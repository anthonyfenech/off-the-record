It was old news, but it came up again last month, when Justin Verlander was quoted as saying the

&ldquo;I knew something was wrong,&rdquo; Verlander told Bleacher Report in an Aug. 9 article. &ldquo;They take me out and misdiagnose me. &mldr; Didn&rsquo;t get an MRI. Because it was so mild.&rdquo;

The revelation left many scratching their heads at how the Tigers&rsquo; training staff could misdiagnose an injury to their franchise player and led Verlander &mdash; who returned to Detroit for the first time as a member of the Astros this week &mdash;

When asked for clarity on the topic before Wednesday afternoon&rsquo;s series finale, Verlander said, &ldquo;I&rsquo;m going to let it lie. No point in drudging up the past anymore,&rdquo; but also said he believed the article put more emphasis on the injury than needed.

&ldquo;I think the easiest way for me to answer it is, it was initially diagnosed as a tricep and it ended up being a lat,&rdquo; he said. &ldquo;Those are the facts. Didn&rsquo;t get an MRI initially, got one later, and it was a lat.&rdquo;

The Tigers have not commented on Verlander&rsquo;s claim, but through a team spokesperson last month, did provide limited details of his injury rehabilitation process. According to the Tigers, Verlander received multiple MRI scans after leaving a spring training start on March 27, both of which confirmed the diagnosis of a right triceps strain.

Verlander experienced a setback after his first simulated game that year, on April 15, and afterwards received an MRI scan. Often times, players are reluctant to receive MRI scans, which afford teams an opportunity to find additional injuries, which could force them to be sidelined longer than they hoped. Less than a week later, Rand &mdash; then the Tigers&rsquo; head athletic trainer &mdash; assessed the results, saying, &ldquo;Turned out everything was as we expected.&rdquo;

On April 28, Verlander explained the reason for a second MRI scan, saying, "Just to assess the healing process and kind of gauge where we were at.&rdquo;

Verlander then received a third MRI scan and during a road trip to Chicago, reviewed the results with the Tigers&rsquo; training staff and orthopedic surgeon Anthony Romeo. The diagnosis was a right lat tear.

It is possible Verlander tore the lat during his rehabilitation process: That season, Verlander was chomping at the bit to return.

Verlander received a seven-year, $180 million contract extension in the spring of 2013, drawing a yearly salary of $28 million &mdash; his health was likely high on the team&rsquo;s list of priorities. The disabled list stint in 2015 was the only DL visit of his career.

After the meeting in Chicago, he was then treated for the right lat injury, returned to action on June 13, and has pitched at a high level ever since: In 2016, Verlander finished second in AL Cy Young Award voting; in 2017, he helped pitch the Astros to their first World Series championship; this season, he has posted a 2.72 ERA and 0.944 ERA in 31 starts.

Verlander returned to Comerica Park on Monday night and struck out 10 batters over seven innings. Two days after the heartfelt return, he made clear there was no lingering frustration over his only stint on the disabled list.

&ldquo;I don&rsquo;t have any ill will towards them,&rdquo; he said. &ldquo;Like I said, I think that article blew it out of proportion. I don&rsquo;t know if the original diagnosis caused anything &mdash; I don&rsquo;t know if it caused me extra time &mdash; I don&rsquo;t know.

&ldquo;This organization did a lot for me and I really don&rsquo;t hold any ill will towards that time. I texted Al and Kevin immediately and they said they both understood. Those words were kind of not taken out of context, but made to seem a little more dramatic.&rdquo;
