---
HEADLINE: Detroit Tigers' Nicholas Castellanos must own up to his mistakes
BYLINE: Anthony Fenech
DATE: 2018-04-15
URL: https://www.freep.com/story/sports/mlb/tigers/2018/04/15/nick-castellanos-detroit-tigers/517908002/
---

Nicholas Castellanos was in right field early Friday afternoon. Then he was in center field. And he nearly ran baseballs down into left field.

The Tigers were taking early batting practice at Comerica Park, and Castellanos, the team&rsquo;s right fielder, was using it as early fielding practice.

He read balls off the bat, ranging left and right and forward and backward.

&ldquo;You don&rsquo;t just turn that overnight and all of a sudden you&rsquo;re going to be a right fielder without work,&rdquo; manager Ron Gardenhire said. &ldquo;You gotta put in the work that he&rsquo;s doing.&rdquo;

Castellanos has been putting in the work, both in the outfield and inside the Tigers&rsquo; clubhouse, since last September, when the Tigers moved him permanently to right field &mdash; and made moves that asserted him as leader. He&rsquo;s learning every day how to be better in both facets.

During last weekend&rsquo;s sweep of the Chicago White Sox, I realized I hadn&rsquo;t paid much attention to Castellanos&rsquo; defense in right field. That&rsquo;s not a bad thing. He&rsquo;s had some misplays and overthrows, but he hasn&rsquo;t been charged with an error, and nothing has been egregious enough to think he cannot succeed in the outfield. He can. During the series opener against the Indians, he made an impressive running catch in the right field gap to take away what looked like a double. He caught it with the sun in his face, showing how the position is becoming second nature to him.

&ldquo;I feel good,&rdquo; he said before the series opener against the Yankees on Friday night. &ldquo;I feel like it&rsquo;s just, the more I play, the more comfortable I&rsquo;m going to get. Really, that&rsquo;s it.&rdquo;

Maybe he lost the ball in the lights. Perhaps he was unsure how much room he had in front of the wall. Either way, after watching multiple replays, Castellanos should have made the catch.

After the game, he was approached by one reporter, then a couple more and soon by a small scrum. They wanted to know what happened on the play.

He didn&rsquo;t lose it in the lights, he said. Sanchez just doubled. It was a line drive off the wall. There was no misplay, he said, it was just him going back, finding the wall and by the time he looked up, the ball already was over his head.

He was asked if the play was part of his transition phase to right field.

&ldquo;I mean, sure,&rdquo; he said. &ldquo;The more experience and the more time I spend out there, the more comfortable I&rsquo;m going to get. But I think it&rsquo;s funny, in 40-degree weather, I get a line drive off the wall that&rsquo;s a double and I get a bunch of microphones and stuff in my face asking me if this is a transition, you know? I think it&rsquo;s funny. But it is what it is.&rdquo;

Hours earlier, Tigers icon Willie Horton sat outside the team&rsquo;s clubhouse sharing baseball stories. Among them was a conversation he had with longtime Seattle baseball scribe Emmett Watson from when he joined the Mariners in 1979. Horton could do no wrong then.

&ldquo;They were clapping their hands for me even when I struck out,&rdquo; he said.

Horton implored Watson to criticize him publicly when it was called for. Horton appreciated the criticism. It motivated him. He still considers Detroit sportswriter Joe Falls, who covered the Tigers for the Free Press in the 1960s and 70s, an influential part of his career for that very reason.

Perhaps Castellanos has grown weary of questions about his defense. After all, he has fielded them since being named starting third baseman for a contending team at 22 years old. Perhaps the Tigers&rsquo; five-game losing streak irritated him. Clearly, he didn&rsquo;t think the play was worthy of interrogation.

Castellanos, seemingly overnight, has become one of this team&rsquo;s leaders. All spring and early this season, he has played the part, showing a willingness to interact with teammates young and old. He displays leadership, though that leadership &mdash; like his defense &mdash; still needs more seasoning.

For as new as he is to right field, he is less experienced at being a leader. It&rsquo;s there, innately like it needs to be. This winter, he spoke of Torii Hunter as someone whose leadership he would like to emulate. There also is a little bit of Ian Kinsler in Castellanos.

But in order to be that kind of leader, he needs to face the questions that Hunter did on a near-nightly basis, no matter how unfair he thinks they are. He needs to take responsibility for mistakes, even if they aren&rsquo;t the difference in wins and losses. Kinsler didn&rsquo;t always give good quotes, but if he screwed up, he&rsquo;d say he screwed up. It&rsquo;s part of the job.

Down the hallway, just minutes before Castellanos spoke, manager Ron Gardenhire reaffirmed his feeling on Castellanos, which has never wavered. Asked if he had any concern with Castellanos&rsquo; defense, Gardenhire said, &ldquo;None. He is our right fielder. He will be all season.&rdquo;

That means there will be no more late-inning defensive substitutions, which chided Castellanos in the past, and even if there are more questions of the sort for Gardenhire, he has made his thoughts crystal clear.

&ldquo;It&rsquo;s great,&rdquo; Castellanos said before the game against the Yankees. &ldquo;It feels great. If you&rsquo;re a competitor, you never want to be taken out of the game.&rdquo;

On Friday night, after the misplay in the second inning and inside a sparse Tigers&rsquo; clubhouse afterwards, the development of both Castellanos the right fielder and Castellanos the leader was on display.

He is, as Gardenhire said before the game, speaking solely about his defense, a work in progress. Maybe next time Castellanos will know how close he is to the wall. Maybe next time he will say he messed up.

Because that&rsquo;s what leaders say.
