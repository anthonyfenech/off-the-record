The Tigers finished 64-98, in last place in the American League Central Division for the second time in three years and tied with the San Francisco Giants for the worst record in baseball. They were not good enough, even with the requisite amount of veteran leadership and manager

In the end, it was a perfect storm of a bloated payroll, bad pitching and big-time regressions from their most important players.

Said second baseman Ian Kinsler, when asked what went wrong this season, &ldquo;I don&rsquo;t know if it ever went right.&rdquo;

All of that is now in the past. The overspending. The prospect-parting. The days of trying to buy their way to a World Series title, future be damned. The Tigers as we knew them left when Avila finally ripped the Band-Aid off a month ago and traded Justin Verlander. In the months that follow, Kinsler likely will find a new home in favor of more prospects whom the Tigers hope will make up the core of their next great team.

For their efforts this September &ndash; going 6-24 &ndash; the team will receive the No. 1 pick in next year&rsquo;s MLB draft. More importantly, they will have the largest bonus pool to spend creatively on amateur players in what will be a crucial draft, just like this season was crucial in beginning to build a sustainable foundation.

Their in-season moves &ndash; trading J.D. Martinez to the Diamondbacks and Justin Wilson and Alex Avila to the Cubs and Justin Upton to the Angels and Verlander to the Astros &ndash; have put the organization in a much better spot than they were at this time last year, when their old-model car couldn&rsquo;t make it across the finish line once again.

In hindsight, the youth movement came too late. But in light of that, Avila proved to move swiftly and beat limited demand with Martinez. He gauged peak value well by moving Wilson for an impressive young player in Jeimer Candelario and holding out for what they deemed to be fair value for Verlander.

The Triple-A-caliber lineups Ausmus penned in until his final game were a product of that foundation being formed: In large part, the Tigers have cashed in their marketable chips. Their farm system is more formidable than any point in recent memory and Avila&rsquo;s strong suit in building an organization through evaluating talent and player development is now full throttle ahead.

It was never going to be easy. The Tigers didn&rsquo;t deteriorate slowly &ndash; warts and all, they finished just 2 &#189; games out of the playoffs last season. They seemed to deteriorate within the span of a month and a half, which made the precipitous fall late in the season feel that much worse.

&ldquo;You&rsquo;ve gotta snap out of it and move forward,&rdquo; Al Avila said. &ldquo;And we&rsquo;re at that point right now, that point where it&rsquo;s kind of exciting.&rdquo;

He used the simplest analogy to describe what happened this season.

&ldquo;It&rsquo;s almost like a death,&rdquo; he said. &ldquo;You mourn and then you come out of it and now you&rsquo;re ready to live again and move on. It&rsquo;s an exaggeration, but it&rsquo;s kind of like that. And right now, we&rsquo;re very excited for a brand new opportunity to revive the organization.&rdquo;

It rained all morning in Minneapolis. It was cool and cloudy and gray. It looked like the Tigers&rsquo; season. But sometime in the early innings, sunshine peeked through those clouds and offered a perspective on things to come.

There will be more clouds and more rain for the next couple seasons. But the sun will shine again on the Tigers and laying the most prosperous stretch in franchise history to rest is the only way blue skies will return to Comerica Park.
