HEADLINE: Erik Bakich, Bo Schembechler, and a Michigan baseball awakening
BYLINE: Anthony Fenech
DATE: 2019-06-15

OMAHA, Neb. &ndash; He has been pulled in different directions since Michigan baseball landed here on Wednesday night, first heading across the tarmac to the Vanderbilt plane to say hello to Tim Corbin, a mentor, and Corbin&rsquo;s wife, Maggie.

The next morning, he&rsquo;s at a local high school, hitting infield practice and signing autographs, taking pictures with toddlers and talking to reporters. He will have a couple hours to relax afterwards, but his schedule is jam-packed: News conferences, photo shoots, the Tigers-Royals game, a morning workout at TD Ameritrade Park, an autograph session, an evening barbeque, the opening ceremonies and however many meet-and-greets and &ldquo;Go Blue!&rdquo; responses.

&ldquo;These first couple days are like a carnival,&rdquo; he says, standing a few feet from a team bus full of  restless college players. &ldquo;I think our players feel like they&rsquo;re part-tourists, part-rock stars and part-baseball players. It&rsquo;s just one event after the next.&rdquo;

It is, Bakich will come to learn, the commotion and celebration of the College World Series, which U-M is playing in for the first time since 1984. The program has been headed in this direction for a few years, bringing in top recruiting classes, sitting near the top of the Big Ten but coming up short in the big spots.

Now, after a walk-off win in the Big Ten tournament put what Bakich called, &ldquo;Magic in a bottle,&rdquo; the Wolverines are here, trying to spring-board their way into perennial contention as the northern-most school against a bunch of warm-weather juggernauts.

Bakich, 41, has been chasing the College World Series as a head coach for more than a decade. He&rsquo;s considered one of the up-and-coming coaches in the country, has been wooed elsewhere, but stayed in Ann Arbor. He has the smallest salary among the head coaches competing in Omaha this week.

And as he signs autographs with the rest of Team 153, a man who knows all about the start of Bakich&rsquo;s coaching journey walks up, pulling him in another direction, back to where it all began, at East Carolina.

George Whitfield remembers the first time he saw Bakich.

&ldquo;I thought, &lsquo;That was a hard-nosed kid,&rsquo;&rdquo; Whitfield says. &ldquo;Right away, I could see his leadership. He wasn&rsquo;t a &lsquo;rah-rah&rsquo; guy &ndash; he was very quiet &ndash; but the boys respected him.&rdquo;

Whitfield was an unpaid assistant on Keith LeClair&rsquo;s East Carolina teams in the late-1990s, getting an up-close-and-personal look at what has become the LeClair coaching tree: They are scattered all over &ndash; Bakich and associate head coach Nick Schnabel at U-M, assistant coach Bryant Ward at UCLA, director of baseball development Ben Sanderson at N.C. State and Dodgers minor-league field coordinator Clayton McCullough &ndash; and all of them wear No. 23 in honor of LeClair, who died years ago from ALS.

&ldquo;I think about Keith LeClair every time I put my jersey on,&rdquo; Bakich says. &ldquo;I think about him every day that I don&rsquo;t put my jersey on. He changed the course of my life and the trajectory.&rdquo;

When he arrived at East Carolina from California in 1999, Bakich thought he was going to college to be a business major and play baseball. But while playing for LeClair, &ldquo;I realized that I wanted to have the same impact on student-athletes that he had on me and my teammates.&rdquo;

Bakich changed his major to sports science, and after playing in the independent leagues for a couple years, followed LeClair&rsquo;s lead to Clemson as a volunteer assistant. Seven years at Vanderbilt with Corbin later, he took the Maryland head coaching job, and three years after that received a call from then-U-M athletic director Dave Brandon, telling him the school would like to interview him for the head coach job.

&ldquo;He left my office and he was our finalist candidate,&rdquo; Brandon said. &ldquo;I was just completely wowed by the energy and thoughtfulness and preparation and the way he presented himself. If we could get him, that was the guy that I wanted to coach our baseball team.&rdquo;

Shortly thereafter, on June 27, 2012, they got him. In the years since, as U-M has established itself as one of the Big Ten&rsquo;s best, other schools have tried to get him. But he stayed, spurning offers from Stanford and South Carolina, citing Bo Schembechler as a reason why.

&ldquo;Those who stay,&rdquo; Bakich says.

They aren&rsquo;t champions yet. But U-M&rsquo;s 5-3 win over Texas Tech in the College World Series opener on Saturday afternoon has put them in prime position, entering the winner&rsquo;s bracket as the liveliest of underdogs, with their ace lefty, Tommy Henry, set to pitch on Monday.

And they might not have even made it this far if it wasn&rsquo;t for Bakich&rsquo;s evolution as a coach. He is ever-detailed, almost OCD-like, those closest to him say, and in years past, perhaps some of the pressure he put on himself carried over to his players, who put up fine regular seasons but had yet to advance past the NCAA regionals.

&ldquo;I think he&rsquo;s figured out the right way to handle a team throughout the whole year,&rdquo; senior infielder Jimmy Kerr said. &ldquo;Especially this year, keeping everyone fresh and loose, he&rsquo;s done an incredible job.&rdquo;

Earlier in the season, when Miles Lewis kept swinging late at fastballs, Bakich repeated, &ldquo;On time,&rdquo; from the dugout. Standing next to him &ndash; he was in the hole &ndash; Kerr told his coach, &ldquo;He knows. It&rsquo;s going to be alright. He doesn&rsquo;t need a reminder.&rdquo;

After the game, Bakich came up to Kerr and said, &ldquo;You know what? You&rsquo;re right. Thanks for that. I know I don&rsquo;t need to tell him.&rdquo;

&ldquo;It&rsquo;s all the right intentions,&rdquo; Kerr said. &ldquo;He wants to put in so much effort and do so much for his guys, so it&rsquo;s way better than having a coach that doesn&rsquo;t care. I would want a guy like him every single day.&rdquo;

Bakich&rsquo;s evolution has coincided with as tightly knit of a team as he&rsquo;s had at U-M. He trusts the players, and in turn, they trust him &ndash; to make them better, motivate them, and oftentimes, make them laugh.

&ldquo;I think being loose at the right time is important,&rdquo; Bakich says. &ldquo;Being loose at the wrong time is a mistake. We&rsquo;re very loose right now and this is the time to be loose. You don&rsquo;t want to be loose in the fall, when you need to foundationally implement things that are culture-based, standards-based and fundamentals-based.&rdquo;

The fall was the time to set a tone. His first assignment to the team was to create a presentation on a Michigan alum.

&ldquo;The impetus of that was to make sure our guys knew that they were simply standing on the shoulders of so many great men and teams,&rdquo; Bakich said. &ldquo;Our collective goal was to add as much value as we possibly could to that and mark our page in the history book that was going to be one that would be bookmarked for all-time.

&ldquo;As the eighth team in 153 years that&rsquo;s advanced this far, I think that page will be bookmarked, but the story behind that is the legacy they wanted to leave, and how it would be an inspiration for future generations of Michigan baseball players and Michigan athletes.&rdquo;

Bakich &ndash; along with Schnabel and pitching coach Chris Fetter &ndash; are dogged recruiters, and know all too well of the challenges they face in getting some of the best high school players in the country to pass on the pros and then come up to often-cold Ann Arbor, where they will play through terrible conditions for much of the season.

The weather, they don&rsquo;t run from. With the athletic department funding a turf field at Ray Fisher Stadium, in the years after Bakich was hired, they are outside every day, weather be damned. They get the graveyard shift inside the Oosterbaan Field House, but don&rsquo;t take it on principle: They are a baseball team and baseball is played outside.

&ldquo;It&rsquo;s definitely a big part of who we are,&rdquo; senior lefty Ben Keizer said. &ldquo;It could be zero degrees outside with a pile of snow, but whether it&rsquo;s 20 minutes to an hour, we get out there and we realize the importance and really embrace that.&rdquo;

It&rsquo;s tough, sure, both mentally and physically. Some days, you can&rsquo;t feel your hands. But one day, when Bakich came out to see his players with their hands in their hoodies and back pockets, he decided to flip the switch and set the tone for the season.

&ldquo;He&rsquo;s getting mad,&rdquo; said senior Jordan Brewer, the Big Ten Player of the Year. &ldquo;&rsquo;Take your hands out of your pants! It&rsquo;s fine!&rsquo; And we&rsquo;re warming up, it&rsquo;s snowing sideways, and he just rips his shirt off. It was the funniest thing.&rdquo;

It was, Bakich says, colder than 20 degrees.

&ldquo;It was a lot colder than that,&rdquo; he says. &ldquo;Sometimes, it&rsquo;s to keep them loose and sometimes, it&rsquo;s to tell them to quit being soft and toughen up &ndash; it&rsquo;s 70 degrees inside your head.&rdquo;

It went back to an e-mail he received from his son&rsquo;s elementary school, reminding parents to send their kids to school with mittens and snowpants and beanies. He read the e-mail to his team.

&ldquo;If the temperature was above zero degrees, they&rsquo;d be having school,&rdquo; Bakich says. &ldquo;So, if the temperature is above zero, we&rsquo;re going outside. It may be only for a few minutes, but if they&rsquo;re going to whine about it, I&rsquo;m probably going to take my shirt off and say, &lsquo;See.&rsquo; &rdquo;

If LeClair could see Bakich now, he surely would be smiling down. The bond between Bakich and the LeClair family has only grown.

With a flight delay likely preventing them from making it to Omaha in time for Saturday&rsquo;s game, LeClair&rsquo;s wife, Lynn, and daughter, Audrey, left the Charlotte airport and drove 17 hours to arrive at TD Ameritrade Field just before game time.

&ldquo;This is definitely a special day for us,&rdquo; Lynn said.

LeClair loved Bakich because he was a &ldquo;Navy Seal-type guy,&rdquo; and saw a little bit of himself in Bakich. He pushed him to coaching because he knew Bakich would be a natural fit, and helped start his ascension by urging legendary Clemson coach Jack Leggett to hire him.

&ldquo;All of them,&rdquo; Lynn said. &ldquo;He would be extremely proud of them.&rdquo;

Last year, after U-M missed out on the NCAA tournament, Bakich and his wife, Jiffy, flew to support East Carolina in their regional matchup.

&ldquo;I&rsquo;m honored that they remember him in this way,&rdquo; Lynn said, &ldquo;and that they want to carry on his vision to Omaha.&rdquo;

And sometimes, if she listens closely, she can hear LeClair in Bakich&rsquo;s words.

&ldquo;It goes back a step beyond that, to Jack Leggett, who was Keith&rsquo;s coach,&rdquo; Lynn said. &ldquo;I actually called him before coming here to let him know that we were coming and Erik, when I hear him speak anyway, sounds like Jack Leggett to me.&rdquo;

And Bakich, named national coach of the year by the National Collegiate Baseball Writers Association on Saturday, doesn&rsquo;t sound like someone who sees himself in anything but maize-and-blue in the future. Though, undoubtedly, schools will try.

&ldquo;If we&rsquo;re going to recruit at the highest level and talk to high-level recruits about not taking money to come to school, then my wife and I didn&rsquo;t want to lead a program where we were jumping for a higher paycheck, as well,&rdquo; he says.

&ldquo;In building a program, if you want to be a perennial powerhouse, stay and build it. And that just so happens to be one of the foundational themes for the University of Michigan.&rdquo;

&ldquo;And it&rsquo;s never rang true more than right now. Had we gone to a different school and went to the College World Series, it wouldn&rsquo;t mean as much as taking the University of Michigan to the College World Series.&rdquo;
